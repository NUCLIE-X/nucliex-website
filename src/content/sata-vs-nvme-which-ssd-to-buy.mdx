Every week someone asks us this in the workshop, usually holding a laptop that
deserves better than the drive inside it. The answer is less dramatic than the
marketing suggests — and it depends on the machine, not the benchmark.

## The short answer

If your machine only has a 2.5-inch bay, buy a SATA SSD and stop reading. If
you're building or upgrading something modern with an M.2 slot that supports
NVMe, buy NVMe — the price difference has mostly disappeared. The complicated
cases are the ones in between, and that's what the rest of this article is for.

## What SATA and NVMe actually mean

SATA and NVMe are interfaces — the road between the drive and the rest of the
computer — not measures of quality.

SATA III was designed in the hard-drive era. Its 6 Gb/s link means real-world
sequential transfers top out around 550 MB/s, no matter how fast the flash
behind it is. Almost every 2.5-inch SSD is SATA.

NVMe was designed for flash from the start. It runs over PCIe lanes, so the
ceiling depends on the PCIe generation: roughly 3,500 MB/s on Gen3 x4, around
7,000 MB/s on Gen4 x4, and higher again on Gen5. NVMe drives usually come in
the M.2 2280 "stick of gum" form factor.

One trap worth knowing: some M.2 slots — especially in older laptops — are
SATA-only, and some M.2 drives are SATA drives in an M.2 shape. The connector
alone doesn't tell you which road you're on. Check the manual, not the socket.

## The numbers, honestly framed

Here is the part spec sheets don't emphasise: for booting Windows, opening a
browser, and loading everyday applications, a SATA SSD and an NVMe drive feel
almost identical. Those workloads are dominated by small random reads, and both
technologies handle them far better than any hard drive ever did.

The gap shows up when you move large amounts of data in one go — video
projects, disk images, game installs, big datasets. Copying a 50 GB file is
several times faster on a good NVMe drive. If your day involves that, the
difference is real money-worth of time. If it doesn't, it's a benchmark you'll
never feel.

## When SATA is the right call

- The machine only has a 2.5-inch bay or a SATA-only M.2 slot. This is most
  laptops older than a few years — and the upgrade is still transformative.
- You're reviving an office fleet. Fifty SATA drives make fifty machines feel
  new; the interface ceiling is irrelevant for spreadsheets and email.
- The drive is a secondary data disk. Documents and media don't need PCIe
  bandwidth.

## When NVMe is worth it

- The machine has a proper NVMe-capable M.2 slot — most desktop boards and
  laptops from the last several years.
- You move big files as part of the job: footage, renders, virtual machines,
  large builds.
- You're building new. There's no reason to give a new machine yesterday's
  interface when the price gap is small.

## The checklist

1. Find out what the machine accepts: 2.5-inch SATA, M.2 SATA, or M.2 NVMe —
   and which PCIe generation if NVMe.
2. Decide from your workload, not the headline number: everyday use is served
   by either; heavy file movement favours NVMe.
3. Compare endurance and warranty, not just speed. A drive is a promise about
   the future — the term and the TBW rating tell you how much future the maker
   stands behind.
4. Buy the capacity you'll actually fill plus room to breathe. A fuller SSD is
   a slower SSD, whatever the interface.
