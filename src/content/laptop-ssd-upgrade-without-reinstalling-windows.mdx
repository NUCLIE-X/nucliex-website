The fastest way to make an ageing laptop feel new is an SSD. The fastest way
to ruin the weekend is doing the swap without a plan. This is the process our
technicians use — clone first, swap second, and verify before anything gets
wiped.

## Before you start

Three rules that prevent almost every disaster:

- **Back up anything you can't lose** before touching hardware. A clone is not
  a backup until it has booted successfully.
- **Don't wipe the old drive** until the new one has run for a few days. It is
  your rollback plan.
- **Windows activation survives a drive swap.** The licence is tied to the
  machine, not the disk — you will not need to reactivate or find a product key.

You'll need the new SSD, a small screwdriver, and a way to connect the new
drive while cloning: a USB-to-SATA cable or M.2 enclosure, whichever matches
the drive you bought.

## Step 1: check what your laptop can take

Open the service manual or the manufacturer's spec page for your exact model
and confirm two things: the physical slot (2.5-inch bay, M.2, or both) and —
for M.2 — whether the slot speaks SATA, NVMe, or either. Buying the wrong kind
of M.2 drive is the most common self-upgrade mistake we see.

While you're there, check the drive you're replacing. If Windows currently
lives on a 500 GB disk that's 300 GB full, the new SSD needs at least that
much used-space headroom.

## Step 2: clone the old drive

Connect the new SSD over USB and clone the entire system disk to it — not just
the C: partition. A Windows installation includes small hidden partitions (EFI
boot, recovery) and the clone must carry all of them.

Use a disk-cloning utility that explicitly supports system disks; most SSD
vendors ship one free with their drives, and several well-known free imaging
tools do the same job. The clone will take from twenty minutes to a few hours
depending on how much data you have.

When it finishes, don't skip verification: the cloning tool should report
success, and the new drive's partition layout should mirror the old one.

## Step 3: swap the drives

Power off fully — shut down, not sleep — unplug the charger, and if the
battery is removable, remove it. Ground yourself on something metal before
touching the internals.

Open the service panel, swap the old drive for the new one, and reassemble.
On most laptops this is four to eight screws; if yours needs the whole bottom
case removed and pried, work slowly at the clips or let a technician do it.

## Step 4: first boot and checks

The machine should boot straight into your Windows exactly as you left it. If
it doesn't find a bootable drive, enter the firmware settings and check the
boot order — the new drive sometimes registers as a new entry.

Once you're in:

1. Confirm Windows sees the full new capacity in Disk Management. If the clone
   left unallocated space, extend the partition into it.
2. Check that TRIM is on: `fsutil behavior query DisableDeleteNotify` should
   return 0.
3. Use the machine normally for a few days with the old drive stored safely.
   Then wipe the old drive and repurpose it as external storage.

## What can go wrong

- **The clone won't boot.** Usually a partition-mode mismatch or an incomplete
  clone. Re-clone with a tool that copies the whole disk including hidden
  partitions.
- **The new drive isn't detected while cloning.** New SSDs sometimes arrive
  uninitialised — initialise the disk in Disk Management first.
- **The laptop resists opening.** Some chassis hide screws under feet or
  keyboards. The service manual knows; force doesn't.
