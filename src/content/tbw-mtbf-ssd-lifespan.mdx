Two numbers dominate every SSD endurance conversation: TBW and MTBF. One is a
fuel tank, the other is a population statistic, and neither is the countdown
timer people assume. Reading them correctly tells you a lot about a drive —
and about the honesty of whoever published them.

## Why these two numbers exist

Flash memory wears out by writing. Each cell tolerates a finite number of
program-erase cycles, so manufacturers need a way to express "how much writing
this drive is built to absorb". That's TBW. Separately, electronics fail for
reasons unrelated to wear — controllers, capacitors, solder joints — and MTBF
is the statistical language for that kind of failure. Two different failure
modes, two different numbers. The confusion starts when either is read as
"how long until my drive dies".

## TBW: the fuel tank

TBW — terabytes written — is the total volume of writes the manufacturer
rates the drive for within its warranty. A 240 TB TBW rating on a 512 GB drive
means the maker stands behind roughly 240 terabytes of cumulative writes.

The way to make that concrete is to divide by your own usage. A typical office
or home machine writes in the range of tens of gigabytes a day at the high
end. At even 40 GB written every single day, 240 TB lasts over sixteen years —
far beyond the useful life of the machine around it. Heavy video work,
databases, or surveillance recording change that maths, which is why those
buyers should do the division before buying, not after.

Two things TBW is not: it isn't a cliff (drives don't die at the rated number;
the rating is where the warranty's write cover ends), and it isn't a promise
about any single drive — it's the engineering margin the maker commits to.

## MTBF: the statistic everyone misreads

MTBF — mean time between failures — is quoted in huge numbers, often a
million hours or more. A million hours is 114 years, which is obviously not a
claim that your drive will outlive your grandchildren.

MTBF describes a population, not a unit. A million-hour MTBF means that if you
ran a thousand identical drives, you'd statistically expect about one failure
per thousand hours across the whole fleet — roughly one failure in the fleet
every six weeks. It's a defect-rate statement in disguise, useful for fleet
planning and meaningless as a personal lifespan prediction.

What it _is_ good for: comparing the claimed baseline reliability of drive
families, and estimating replacement rates when you run many machines.

## DWPD: the same idea in different units

Enterprise spec sheets often quote DWPD — drive writes per day — instead of
TBW. It's the same fuel tank expressed as a rate: how many times you can write
the drive's full capacity every day for the warranty period. The conversion is
simple: DWPD = TBW ÷ (capacity × warranty days). A consumer drive might work
out around 0.2–0.3 DWPD; write-heavy enterprise drives are rated at 1, 3, or
more. If you're comparing a consumer and an enterprise drive, convert one
number into the other and you'll usually find the difference is the whole
point of the price gap.

## How to actually use these numbers

1. **Estimate your daily writes.** Operating system telemetry and SMART data
   (the "host writes" counter) tell you what you actually write, which is
   usually less than people guess.
2. **Divide TBW by that figure.** If the answer is decades, endurance is not
   your buying criterion — move on to warranty term and price.
3. **Treat MTBF as a fleet number.** Buying one drive? Weight the warranty
   terms and the maker's RMA behaviour far more heavily.
4. **Watch the drive, not the rating.** SMART attributes report wear as it
   happens. A drive that reports its health honestly beats a bigger number on
   a sticker.

The pattern behind all of it: endurance ratings are commitments, and they're
only as good as the company behind the warranty. Read the numbers, then read
the RMA process — the second one tells you what happens when the first one is
tested.
